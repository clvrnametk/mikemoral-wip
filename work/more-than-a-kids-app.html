Engagement improved where decline was expected

The situation

Disney+ launched with the strongest brand in family entertainment. That strength became the constraint. To most American households, Disney+ meant one thing: the place you put the kids.

Meanwhile, the business was consolidating around it. Hulu moved in. ESPN followed. Suddenly one product had to hold three of the strongest content catalogs in the world, each with powerful sub-brands of its own, and serve everything from Bluey to Shogun to Monday Night Football.

The question was not how to redesign an app. The question was how to change what Disney+ means to a household while a hundred million people were actively using it.

The problem beneath the problem

You cannot reposition a product with a marketing campaign. What Disney+ means to a household is decided by what happens every time someone opens the app. What shows up first. How content is organized. Whether a sports fan, a prestige-drama viewer, and a six-year-old can each find their version of the product without getting in each other's way.

That made this a systems problem expressed through dozens of product decisions. Navigation. Metadata. Browse. Live. Each one looks like a feature. Together they are an identity.

The work

We rebuilt the foundations of the logged-in experience as one connected effort. We modernized the UI. We redefined the metadata structure underneath the catalog so three libraries could coexist without flattening into mush. We brought video into browse, changing discovery from reading posters to experiencing content. We built Live into the product, because a household that watches sports does not think of games as an add-on.

The navigation evolution was the bet that tested the whole thesis. Major navigation changes are where streaming products go to bleed engagement, and the expectation was that key metrics would take a hit in exchange for the strategic foundation. We made the bet anyway.

Engagement improved.

What changed

Disney+ now serves the whole household, not a segment of it. Sports, general entertainment, and family viewing live in one experience that holds three catalogs without losing coherence. The work that was supposed to cost engagement grew it.

What persists

The metadata structure, the navigation model, and the browse foundations are now the rails that future experiences get built on. That is the part I care about most. Features get replaced. Foundations compound.

The lesson is the same one that shows up everywhere in my work: the hardest problems are rarely design problems. This one looked like an app redesign. It was an identity decision, made one product call at a time.
