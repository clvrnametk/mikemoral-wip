A system for launching designers

The situation

The students were not kids. They were adults who had left careers in finance, healthcare, and education and taken real financial risk to start over. They had ten weeks, five days a week, to go from no design background to a portfolio that would get them hired. The bar was not "learn UX." The bar was "get a job." That distinction changes everything about how you teach.

A course can succeed at delivering content and still fail every student in the room, because completion is not the goal - placement is. So the real problem was never the lectures. It was building something repeatable that reliably turned beginners into hireable designers, cohort after cohort, including the ones who fell behind.

The bet - treat it as a system, not a series of lectures

I started by picking up the curriculum that already existed at General Assembly. Then I rebuilt on top of it - modernizing the lessons, pulling directly from what I was seeing in industry and putting it in front of students the same week. The university certificate programs I also taught in moved slowly; their curriculum had been lapped by the field. The immersive had to be the opposite: current, practical, and constantly rebuilt.

The shift that mattered was treating the whole thing as a system with parts that had to work together - not a stack of slides. Five things had to hold at once: a curriculum that built skill in the right order, a way to teach that made people do rather than watch, assessment honest enough to be useful, a humane way to catch the students who were slipping, and an engine pointed at the actual outcome, a job.

The system

A curriculum that built in sequence. Five units, each ending in a real project: design thinking and research, feature integration, responsive design, shipping design, and a live client project. Skills compounded - nothing was introduced before the thing it depended on. Each unit carried its own briefs, exercises, and deliverables so the difficulty ramped instead of spiking.

A way to teach that made people do. The framework underneath every lesson was simple: I do, we do, you do. I demonstrate, we work through it together, you try it alone. The discipline is balance - how much time the instructor talks versus how much students spend doing - and knowing when to model, when to practice together, and when to let someone struggle productively. I built compression checks into lessons: small honest questions to find out whether people were actually following before moving on, instead of assuming it landed.

Assessment that was actually useful. Every project was scored against a clear rubric, with structured feedback templates so the signal was consistent and specific rather than vague encouragement. Students always knew where they stood and what would move them up.

A humane way to catch people slipping. The part I am proudest of: a formal performance-support process for students falling behind - a written support plan and a clear agreement about what had to change and by when. It was direct and supportive at the same time, designed to give someone a real path back rather than letting them quietly fail. This was performance management before I had the title for it.

An engine pointed at the outcome. The system optimized for getting hired, not for finishing. Mock interviews, an interview-prep pipeline, portfolio reviews, and individual coaching ran alongside the curriculum so that "ready to be hired" was something we built toward deliberately, not hoped for at the end.

What persists

I do not manage the way I did before I taught. The classroom forced me to sequence, to prioritize, to find the one thing that unlocks the next three, and to intervene early and honestly when someone is struggling - because when your only job is to help someone grow, you cannot hide behind the product.

The career-launching system became my operating model for developing people. Structure over improvisation, sequence over information dumps, honest early intervention over hoping it works out, and a single measure of success: is the person in front of me more capable than they were before. The job was never to build an empire. It was to build people - and to build the system that does it repeatably.
